Changing a design is relatively easy while it remains a drawing or model. It becomes much harder once the design has been turned into a building.

This project examined a simple question: **when is it worth spending more on a prototype to reduce the risk of expensive changes later?**

I compared several approaches to architectural prototyping, from paper and modular models to 3D printing and virtual reality. I then used publicly reported cost estimates to explore the relationship between early design work and later renovation.

## The cost of changing your mind

A building passes through a long sequence of decisions before construction begins. Requirements are gathered, plans are drawn, technical details are developed, approvals are obtained, and contractors translate the final documents into physical structures.

Changes can be introduced throughout this process, but their consequences are not equal.

Moving a wall in a digital plan may take minutes. Moving the same wall after construction may affect flooring, wiring, insulation, plumbing, labour, and the use of adjacent rooms.

The later a problem is discovered, the more of the completed work may need to be reconsidered.

## Why prototypes matter

People frequently find it difficult to describe exactly what they want before seeing a possible design. A floor plan may communicate dimensions accurately while remaining difficult for a non-specialist to imagine as a complete space.

Prototypes give clients and designers something more concrete to discuss.

Different forms of prototyping offer different advantages:

- **Drawings** are inexpensive and easy to revise, but require experience to interpret.
- **Digital models** are flexible and detailed, but depend on software skills and screen-based presentation.
- **Physical models** make spatial relationships more tangible, but may be slow or expensive to rebuild.
- **Modular kits** allow rapid rearrangement, but limit the forms and materials that can be represented.
- **Virtual and augmented reality** can present a design at human scale, but require hardware, software, and suitable interaction techniques.

The relevant question is therefore not whether prototyping is valuable in general. It is how much fidelity and effort a particular decision requires.

## Comparing early and late costs

I collected publicly available estimates for prototyping tools, architectural models, software, and residential renovation. I then constructed example scenarios comparing the cost of exploring a design before construction with the cost of changing it afterward.

For example, a simple digital kitchen-planning tool may require little direct expense. A custom physical model or high-fidelity virtual walkthrough requires more time, equipment, and expertise. However, even an expensive prototype may remain inexpensive relative to removing and rebuilding a completed kitchen.

In one illustrative scenario, I estimated that a planning and prototyping process costing approximately $2,000 could potentially prevent a renovation costing approximately $20,000.

This was not a prediction that every prototype would save $18,000. It was a way to make the asymmetry visible: an early representation can be discarded, while a completed structure must be dismantled.

## Prototyping as communication

The analysis led to a broader observation. A prototype does not create value simply by existing. The prototype must help the people involved notice, discuss, and resolve a possible mismatch.

A highly detailed model can still fail if:

- clients cannot understand the representation;
- important requirements were never collected;
- the model emphasizes appearance while hiding practical constraints;
- stakeholders interpret the same prototype differently;
- the most expensive decisions are represented too late.

This makes prototyping part of requirements work. The prototype acts as a shared object through which clients, designers, and builders can test whether they mean the same thing.

## More adaptable prototypes

One promising direction was modularity.

A physical model assembled from replaceable sections can accommodate changes without rebuilding the entire object. Modular prototypes sacrifice some geometric freedom, but make iteration faster and less wasteful.

Digital systems offer a related advantage. Components can be changed, compared, and restored without physically reconstructing the complete model.

A useful future tool might combine both approaches:

- tangible components for direct spatial reasoning;
- a digital model for precise dimensions and alternatives;
- automatic detection of conflicts or constraints;
- an immersive view for understanding the design at human scale.

The goal would not be to create the most realistic prototype possible. It would be to create the least expensive representation capable of exposing an important mistake.

## Limits of the analysis

This was an exploratory course project, not a controlled economic study.

The cost estimates came from different public sources and represented different project types, regions, and levels of detail. The scenarios did not account fully for labour, training, equipment reuse, financing, construction delays, or changes in material prices.

The central estimate should therefore not be read as a general savings ratio.

The project instead helped me formulate a design question that continued into my later work: **how can a small, revisable representation help people understand and make decisions about something much larger?**

In this project, that question concerned buildings and construction costs. In my later research, it became a question about miniature representations, tangible objects, and interaction across different physical and virtual scales.
