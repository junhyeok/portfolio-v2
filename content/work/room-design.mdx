Before moving a sofa, buying a table, or rebuilding a kitchen, people often begin with a smaller representation of the room.

That representation might be a floor plan, a rough sketch, an online planner, or a model assembled from physical objects. Each reduces a full-sized environment into something that can be viewed and changed from above.

But these tools do not simply represent the same design in different forms. They change how people approach the design itself.

In this study, 30 participants designed a living room and a kitchen using one of three tools:

- an online room planner;
- pen and paper;
- LEGO bricks.

I observed how participants worked, asked them to describe their experience, and examined where each tool helped or constrained the design process.

## Why compare these tools?

Professional architects and designers learn how to interpret plans, models, measurements, and scaled representations. Most people do not receive that training, even though they regularly make decisions about rooms and furniture.

Simplified tools make room planning more accessible, but each asks the user to think differently.

An online planner provides correctly sized objects and precise editing controls. Paper begins as a blank surface with few restrictions. LEGO turns the design into something physical that can be assembled and rearranged.

I wanted to understand what people gained and lost when using each approach.

## The study

Each participant designed two rooms for a household with two adults and two children:

- an 18 × 12 foot living room;
- a 12 × 12 foot kitchen.

Participants received the room dimensions and a basic list of fixtures, but could decide what furniture to use, where to place it, and how much detail to include.

The sessions were conducted remotely. Participants using paper and LEGO worked on a printed grid, while online participants used an existing room-planning website. Each session lasted approximately one hour.

Afterward, participants selected words describing the experience, discussed those choices in an interview, and completed a questionnaire about involvement in the activity. I also reviewed recordings of the design sessions to identify recurring behaviours.

## Online: precise and easy to revise

The online planner gave participants a catalogue of furniture with predefined dimensions.

Participants frequently browsed alternatives, placed objects temporarily, and replaced one version with another. A sofa could be exchanged for a loveseat. An object could be resized, rotated, moved, or deleted without reconstructing the rest of the room.

This made the online tool particularly useful for refinement.

Participants also described the interface as organized. Furniture was grouped into categories, and the program provided immediate information about object dimensions.

That structure came with a cost. Participants could only use objects and variations supported by the program. The tool made realistic placement easier, but it also narrowed what could be represented.

## Paper: freedom with hesitation

Paper offered the most open-ended starting point.

Participants could draw any object, change its shape, add annotations, and include details that might not exist in a predefined catalogue. Several participants described this freedom as empowering.

At the same time, a blank page could be intimidating. Some participants were concerned about their drawing ability, even though the study did not evaluate artistic skill.

Their uncertainty appeared in the drawing process. Participants sometimes hovered over the paper before making a mark or began with light lines before committing with more pressure. Once an object was drawn, participants rarely removed it completely. They were more likely to extend a line, write a label, or add details around the existing drawing.

Paper therefore supported rapid ideation, but revising a committed decision required more effort than it did in the online planner.

## LEGO: designing by assembling

LEGO made room planning physical.

Participants searched for pieces, combined them into furniture, and moved the resulting objects around the grid. Colour and shape often became part of the representation. Green pieces became plants, transparent pieces became windows, and combinations of bricks became sofas, counters, or appliances.

Two construction patterns appeared.

Some participants first collected all the pieces needed for an object and then assembled them. Others selected and attached pieces one at a time, allowing the object to develop incrementally.

Participants frequently described the process as engaging, familiar, playful, and satisfying. The bricks made design feel approachable, but they also constrained what could be built. Participants were limited by the sizes, colours, and shapes they happened to have available.

## The problem of scale

The online planner contained furniture with realistic default dimensions. Paper and LEGO did not.

Although the printed grid included a scale conversion, participants sometimes represented furniture at substantially different sizes.

I examined the sofa because every participant included one. The online condition produced relatively consistent sofa dimensions because the objects came from a predefined catalogue. Paper and LEGO representations varied considerably more.

![sofa comparison](/images/room-design/design_scale_example_sofa_wide.png)

*The same type of object could occupy very different amounts of space depending on the tool.*

This was not simply a problem of carelessness. Paper and LEGO required participants to perform a difficult mental translation:

> How large should a full-sized sofa become when represented on this grid?

A participant could create a recognizable sofa and still make it much too small for the intended room. LEGO added another constraint because the available brick dimensions did not always correspond neatly to the desired object.

Miniatures provide a useful overview, but the act of miniaturizing introduces its own design problem.

## Equally engaging for different reasons

Across the three conditions, participants often selected **engaging** and **satisfying** to describe their experience.

The reasons differed.

Online participants valued immediate feedback and the ease of moving objects. Paper participants described the thinking and planning process. LEGO participants emphasized physical construction, problem-solving, and play.

Paper participants selected **empowering** more frequently, often connecting the feeling to producing a design without professional assistance. Online participants more often selected **organized**, while LEGO participants emphasized familiarity and hands-on involvement.

The questionnaire measuring involvement in the activity did not identify a statistically significant difference between the three conditions. This suggests that no single tool was simply “more engaging.” Instead, each supported a different kind of engagement.

## Different tools for different stages

Participants did not necessarily want one tool to replace the others.

Paper was useful for quickly exploring an initial idea. Its flexibility supported rough layouts before dimensions and details were settled.

The online planner became more valuable when precision and repeated editing mattered. Objects could be replaced, resized, and compared without redrawing the complete room.

LEGO provided a tangible way to reason about the arrangement, but worked best when suitable pieces were available.

A possible workflow therefore moves between representations:

1. sketch freely on paper;
2. arrange and discuss ideas with physical objects;
3. translate the design into a digital system;
4. refine measurements and details;
5. inspect the result from a three-dimensional or full-scale perspective.

The better question may not be “Which tool is best?” but “When should the design move from one tool to another?”



## What a hybrid tool could offer

An ideal room-design system might combine:

- the freedom of drawing;
- the physical immediacy of LEGO;
- the measurements and revision tools of software;
- several views of the same design;
- automatic support for maintaining realistic scale.

For example, someone might arrange physical objects on a table while a digital system recognizes them, checks their dimensions, and displays the resulting room at full scale in virtual or augmented reality.

The physical objects would support direct manipulation and experimentation. The digital system would preserve measurements, provide alternatives, and reveal errors that are difficult to notice in miniature form.

This does not mean making every representation more realistic. A rough sketch is useful partly because it remains unfinished. The system should preserve that flexibility while introducing precision only when precision becomes useful.

## Limitations

The study was conducted remotely, so participants used their own paper, drawing tools, computers, and LEGO collections. These differences made the study more representative of everyday materials, but reduced experimental control.

Participants who already owned LEGO were also likely to be familiar with it. Similarly, someone’s regular drawing habits or computer setup may have influenced the experience.

The study compared different participants in each condition rather than having everyone use all three tools. It therefore captured broad differences among the tools, but could not directly determine which tool each individual would prefer after trying all three.

The participants were also relatively highly educated, and several had some experience with room planning, architectural software, or 3D modelling. The findings should not be treated as representing every possible non-expert user.

## Looking back

The study began as a comparison of three ordinary design tools. It ultimately exposed a larger tension.

Physical and open-ended tools support freedom, familiarity, and personal expression. Digital tools support precision, realistic scale, and reversible editing. Improving one side often weakens the other.

That tension became the starting point for my later research.

Instead of asking people to choose between a physical miniature and a digital model, I began investigating how the two could operate together: a tangible representation for direct interaction, a virtual environment for scale and flexibility, and an interaction technique that allows someone to move between overview and full-scale experience.
