I developed a custom finger-tracking glove to support XR studies involving tangible proxies. The goal was to track precise fingertip movements and align a virtual hand representation with a user's real hand in real time.

![Final system](/images/finger-tracking-glove/nailring_5working.png)
*Final finger-tracking glove with nail-ring marker attachments.*

## Motivation

Several studies required accurate finger tracking while interacting with tangible objects. Commercial systems exist, but I wanted a solution that was inexpensive, maintainable, and customizable across multiple projects.

## Early Exploration

An early prototype investigated whether OptiTrack's built-in hand-tracking algorithm could be used directly. The approach relied on asymmetric arrangements of passive markers across the hand and fingers.

Unfortunately, passive tracking frequently suffered from marker swaps and orientation flips, which introduced tracking instability.

## Simplified Active-Marker Tracking

To gain complete control over the tracking pipeline, I implemented a simplified finger-tracking system in Unity using active markers.

![Working glove prototype](/images/finger-tracking-glove/wires_glove.png)

A work glove was used to mount:

- active markers
- a controller PCB
- a battery pack

This design provided reliable tracking performance but introduced two practical challenges:

1. Accommodating different hand sizes.
2. Preserving tactile feedback at the fingertips.

## Nail Ring Design

To avoid covering the finger pads, I experimented with nail-mounted marker attachments.

![Nail ring concept](/images/finger-tracking-glove/using_finger_nailring.png)

The final design used:

- a half-finger glove
- five nail rings
- active markers
- a hand-mounted PCB and battery

![Final prototype](/images/finger-tracking-glove/nailring_5working.png)

This approach maintained tactile feedback while providing stable tracking.

## Outcome

The final system delivered tracked fingertip positions for all five fingers. Marker positions were streamed into Unity and used to drive the virtual hand representation.

Because the virtual hand could be manipulated independently from the tangible proxy, the system enabled experiments involving scale changes and physical-virtual mismatch conditions.
